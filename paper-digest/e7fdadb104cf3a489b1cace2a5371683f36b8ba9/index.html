01 / The puzzle

One jump.
Two stories.

A crime count can rise because crime changed—or because the counting rules changed. In the recorded series, those causes can look identical.

So a detector can point to when the numbers shifted. To learn why, the analyst needs evidence about the register itself.

Core idea · signal ≠ explanation

PELT is the primary detector. SeedBS is a secondary method check. The panel test is a triage signal, not a causal test.

03 / PELT, in plain language

Where would you
put the dividers?

Imagine a row of number-blocks. A little robot can split the row into flatter groups. But every divider costs a coin.

PELT picks the split pattern with the smallest total: leftover wobble + divider costs. Too cheap, and it over-splits. Too expensive, and it misses real steps.

The “pruned” trick? It crosses losing split plans off the list, so it can find the best cuts quickly.

PELT = Pruned Exact Linear Time
Paper, p. 8 · Section 3.5.1
Toy example · not a paper result
Drag, or use the arrow keys. Lower cost allows more cuts; higher cost asks for stronger evidence before splitting.
Two dividers: after block 4 and block 8. The robot finds three fairly flat groups.

This tiny demo uses simple mean-level wobble. The paper’s PELT analysis uses an RBF cost; PELT’s exact dynamic-programming search also prunes losing split candidates to stay fast.

05 / False-positive control

Many checks mean
more lucky alarms.

A false positive is a false alarm: a year gets flagged even though there is no real excess synchrony there.

This paper checks 25 years. Some may look unusual just by luck. BH-FDR adjusts for that pile of checks and aims to keep the expected false-alarm share among the years called at 5% or less, under the method’s assumptions. It cannot promise zero mistakes.

FDR = false discovery rate
Paper, pp. 12–13 · Section 3.9.2
One real example · 2019

Each dot is one annual test. Gray means “not flagged,” not “proved unchanged.”

q = 0.1850After BH-FDR, 2019 is above 0.05, so it is not called.

Raw p = 0.0296 looked small on its own; adjusted q = 0.1850 accounts for checking the year grid. In plain terms: one lucky-looking result did not clear the wider check.

06 / Detection ≠ attribution

A flag starts
the investigation.

The panel test labels timing patterns. It cannot tell whether a synchronized jump came from a new rule, a shared real-world shock, or coincidence.

The author checks dated laws and Police Headquarters gazette records separately. A date match alone is not enough: the source must fit the affected article or counting scope.

07 / Method check

No detector
wins everywhere.

In controlled Gaussian simulations, PELT caught more weak shared shifts but had more false alarms. SeedBS was more conservative, and often missed shifts.

These rates depend on the paper’s simulation design. They are not universal rankings, and real register panels lack complete labels for estimating exhaustive error rates.

09 / The unresolved 2019 cluster

A signal, but
not one answer.

The 2019 year did not pass the panel’s false-positive correction. Yet two individual article counts—unauthorized computer access (267) and false documents (273)—showed statistically significant jumps even after accounting for earlier trends; their causes remain undocumented.

Article 299 has a separate 2018 anti-money-laundering source match. The 2019 cluster mixes explained and unexplained changes; it is not a single finding.

10 / The paper’s takeaway

So—did crime
go up?

Not in one straight line. The rate rose, fell, and levelled off; a short decline coincided with a 2013 counting-methodology break, then the rate rose again to 2021.

Some individual offense categories rose sharply too. But recorded counts reflect both what happened and how the system records it. The paper’s safest conclusion: read each trend within its measurement regime, and check big jumps against source records.
